The short answer

Newborns take small amounts. The CDC says to start a formula-only baby on 1 to 2 ounces (30 to 60 ml) every 2 to 3 hours in the first days, and to give more if they show signs of hunger. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says that by the end of the first month most babies take at least 3 or 4 ounces (90 to 120 ml) per feed, and by 6 months 6 to 8 ounces (180 to 240 ml) at each of 4 or 5 feeds a day.

The AAP also says a baby should usually drink no more than an average of about 32 ounces (960 ml) of formula in 24 hours.

These are typical ranges. The CDC says every baby is different, and how much and how often your baby feeds depends on their needs.

Why the numbers are ranges

The AAP says that no book or website can tell you precisely how much or how often your baby needs to be fed. The table shows what is typical, not a target for each feed.

How much formula per day?

Two AAP rules apply at any age.

Rule (AAP, any age)Amount
By body weight, on averageAbout 2½ oz (75 ml) a day for every pound (453 g)
Usual maximumNo more than an average of about 32 oz (960 ml) in 24 hours

The AAP adds that your baby will probably regulate their own intake, so let them tell you when they have had enough. It says there are high and low limits: if your baby consistently seems to want more or less, discuss it with your pediatrician.

The same AAP page gives both 6 to 8 ounces at each of 4 or 5 feeds by 6 months and the 32 ounce usual maximum, and at the top of that range those feeds would add up to more than 32 ounces.

The CDC says that as your baby gradually eats more solid foods, they will gradually need less formula. For 8 to 12 months, the AAP puts formula at roughly 24 ounces (720 ml) a day. See starting solids.

Signs your baby is hungry, and signs they are full

The CDC lists these signs for babies from birth to 5 months.

Signs of hunger

  • Puts their hands to their mouth.
  • Turns their head toward the breast or bottle.
  • Puckers, smacks or licks their lips.
  • Has clenched hands.

The AAP adds sticking the tongue out, rooting, opening the mouth and fussiness. The CDC says crying is often a late sign of hunger, and the AAP and the NHS call it a late sign.

Signs of fullness

  • Closes their mouth.
  • Turns their head away from the breast or bottle.
  • Relaxes their hands.

The AAP says a baby who becomes fidgety or easily distracted during a feed is probably finished. A baby who drains the bottle and keeps smacking their lips might still be hungry.

Does my baby need to finish the bottle?

No. The CDC says your child does not need to finish a bottle. The NHS says to feed your baby when they seem hungry and not to worry if they do not finish it.

The NHS says not to force a baby to take more milk than they want, because this may be distressing and can lead to overfeeding. If you want to slow a feed down, see paced bottle feeding.

Signs your baby may be getting too little

  • Wet diapers (AAP): 2 to 3 a day in the first few days after birth. After the first 4 to 5 days, at least 5 to 6 a day.
  • Wet diapers (NHS): around 6 a day from a few days after birth, wet with clear or pale yellow urine, or feeling heavy.
  • Poop: the NHS says that within the first week your baby should pass pale yellow or yellowish brown poo, at least once a day.
  • Growth: the AAP says your baby's progress on the growth chart is one way to tell whether they are getting enough. The growth percentiles guide explains how to read one.

The AAP says that if your baby is having trouble gaining weight, you should not wait too long between feeds, even if it means waking your baby. The NHS says that if your baby is under 8 weeks old and has not pooed for 2 to 3 days, talk to your midwife, health visitor or GP, particularly if your baby is gaining weight slowly. Signs your baby may be getting too much

The AAP says bottle-fed babies may be more likely to overfeed, because a bottle is faster and takes less effort. They may keep sucking instead of pulling away when full.

According to the AAP, overfed babies can have stomach pains, gas, spit up or vomit. It says it is better to offer less, since you can always give more if your baby wants it.

The NHS says it is normal for babies to bring up a little milk during or just after a feed. It says to talk to your health visitor or GP if it happens a lot, if your baby is violently sick, seems to be in pain, or if you are worried for any other reason. The AAP says to talk with your pediatrician if your baby wants to eat all the time, even when they are full.

Do the US and UK agree?

On cues, yes. On numbers, the AAP and CDC give ounce ranges by age, while the NHS pages on formula feeding give no fixed amounts. They say "The amount will vary from baby to baby" and point to weight gain and diapers instead.
